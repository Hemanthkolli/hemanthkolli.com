Why I write Architecture Decision Records

15 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · #architecture #adr

Six months after a decision, nobody remembers why it was made. The person who made it may have moved on. The reasons were only spoken out loud, in a meeting nobody wrote down.

An Architecture Decision Record (ADR) fixes that. It is one short document for each big decision. "Architecture" here means the big-picture design of a software system: which tools we use and how the parts fit together.

Think of a ship's log. The crew doesn't note every turn of the wheel. But they do write down each real change of course, and why. Anyone who joins later can read back and see how the ship got where it is.

The format I use

Every record answers the same four questions. Here is my template, with a real-looking title. (AWS CDK is a tool for setting up cloud services with code, but any decision would do.) 

# ADR-0007: Use AWS CDK for new platform stacks

## Status
Accepted

## Context
What problem are we solving? What constraints apply?

## Decision
What we chose, in one or two sentences.

## Consequences
What gets easier, what gets harder, what we must watch.

The status shows where a record is in its life. Proposed means we're still discussing it. Accepted means we build on it. Superseded means a newer record has replaced it.

Tip

Keep the records in the same place as the code, for example in a folder called docs/adr/. Then a new decision gets reviewed in the same pull request as the change it explains. (A pull request is a proposed change that teammates read and approve before it goes in.)

What makes a good ADR

  • One decision per record. If it needs two headings, it's two ADRs.
  • The options you said no to. These are often the most valuable part. They show the next person what was already considered.
  • Honest consequences. Every choice has a cost. Write it down.
  • No edits once accepted. If the decision changes, write a new ADR that replaces the old one. Like a ship's log, you add a new entry; you don't tear out old pages.

What it changed for us

In the architecture groups I've been part of, ADRs gave us a shared place to talk through trade-offs before building anything. A trade-off is what you give up to get something else, like picking a cheaper option that is slower.

They also gave a quick answer to every new team member who asked, "Why is it like this?" Writing them takes a little time. That time is small compared with the meetings they save.

If a decision is worth arguing about, it's worth writing down.
